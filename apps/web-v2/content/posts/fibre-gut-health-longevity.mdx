## Why our grandparents' approach to using foods as medicine worked

Many of us recall homemade childhood medicines curing everything from simple head colds to more troublesome infections using whole foods. Our parents learned these remedies from their parents and their grandparents from theirs.

For centuries, people have known that certain foods, as well as periods without food, are two natural and powerful mechanisms for keeping our bodies healthy. Today, we have data and evidence to prove it: a nutrient-dense diet not only provides the micro and macronutrients to help manage and treat disease, but gives us our most important mechanism to prevent it in the first place.(1)

## Macronutrients and the power of whole foods

Eating for good, long-term health, starts with eating the optimal amounts of nutrients that our body needs to function at its best. Some nutrients can be produced by our body on its own. When our skin is exposed to sunlight, it produces a certain amount of vitamin D. Similarly, some bacteria in our gut can produce vitamin K.(2) However, most nutrients that are essential for our bodies to work well are best sourced from healthy foods that we have the power to choose to eat daily.

For our cells to function — that is for us to have energy to do our daily jobs — our bodies rely on three non-negotiable sources of macronutrients: protein, carbohydrates and fats. Proteins provide essential amino acids needed to build muscle, repair tissues and manufacture vital hormones that signal our bodies what they need. For example, the much-discussed GLP-1 is a hormone that signals to our brain when we are full. Carbohydrates break down into glucose to fuel our daily physical activities and our cognitive demands. Glucose is our brain's favourite food, but too much can lead to overstimulation and unhealthy eating patterns. Fats are our most concentrated source of energy, helping our body absorb fat-soluble vitamins, supporting brain health, and adding flavour to our meals.(3)

True metabolic efficiency comes from all three groups, particularly when delivered in the most wholesome way — meaning we eat whole foods, and importantly, we eat those foods *whole*.

## The true longevity nutrient: fibre

Chewing foods in their intact, unprocessed form (like whole grains instead of milled flour) slows us down. This not only promotes mindful eating, but also physically slows the absorption of glucose — both of which are key to keeping our bodies healthy. The king of this 'slowing down' process is *fibre*.

Fibre is a type of complex carbohydrate. Most of it won't get absorbed, so it doesn't heavily impact glucose levels.(4) Instead, fibre passes through our digestive tract where it sends a signal to our brain that we are full. In fact, eating dietary fibre is one way to naturally increase your body's own GLP-1 production. Additionally, fibre is the main and most preferred food source for the good bacteria in our gut.

## The battle of microbes

When Hippocrates said *"all diseases begin in the gut"*, he already knew that the gut is not merely a digestive tube. It is the source of our body's defence network, where 70% to 80% of our immune cells live.(5)

Filling our plate with whole, fibre-rich foods means we are directly feeding beneficial microbes. When we give them food they want, they naturally grow and multiply and engage in a process called competitive exclusion. Feeding these good microbes is an important longevity mechanism: when these good bugs are hungry, they send signals through the gut-brain axis to be fed and we naturally start to want more healthy, fibre rich foods. Overpopulating good microbes is a good thing because the more we have, the more they can consume available nutrients. Plus they leave no room for inflammatory bacteria to survive.(6)

Understanding this microbial competition changes everything. We often blame a lack of willpower for our cravings, but science shows us that our cravings are heavily influenced by the microbes we feed. Just as good bacteria want to eat fibre, bacteria that thrive on ultra-processed foods and refined sugars can send signals demanding more of the unhealthy food they need to survive.(7)

To win the battle for our microbes and our health, we have guidelines that recommend eating 30g fibre a day.(8) In day to day life, that means building our plate around natural foods in their whole form. The good news is, fibre is available in many seasonal and approachable foods such as legumes, apples, kiwis, raspberries, pears, nuts, psyllium husks, cucumbers, celery, carrots, artichokes, chicory, radicchio, and mushrooms.

Starting simple is key. Simply add more fibre-rich foods to your plate with each meal. Over time, your palate physically shifts. Your gut and microbes can do the heavy lifting, helping you to stop thinking about diets or rules because your body naturally begins to crave the very foods that sustain it. This is where healthy eating transitions from a daily cognitive battle into a seamless, natural habit.
