> *"The need for connection and community is primal, as fundamental as the need for air, water, and food." — Dean Ornish*

## Why human connection matters more than you think

Have you ever noticed how spending time with a cheerful person can lift your mood? Or how negativity can spread through a group?

We are wired to feel the emotions of those around us. The feelings and behaviours of your friends, and even your friends' friends — up to three degrees of separation — can influence your own emotional state and health habits.(1)

The connections we build with family, friends, colleagues, and even strangers can have a profound impact on our emotional wellbeing, physical health, and even how long we live. Connection is not merely a social luxury; it is a basic human need.

## Connection in the age of social media

With smartphones, social media, and instant messaging, we have access to more people than at any other time in history. Yet many of us report feeling more isolated, disconnected, and lonely than ever before.(2) ~16 million people in the UK report feeling lonely either "often, always or some of the time".(3) This raises an important question: if we are so connected digitally, why do we often feel so disconnected emotionally?

We often speak of connection as though it lives only in the mind — a feeling, a thought, an emotion. But the body knows otherwise. The moment a newborn is placed skin-to-skin on their parent's chest, their heart rate steadies, their breathing slows, their stress hormones fall. This is connection doing what it was always designed to do — regulate us.(4) And this capacity does not disappear as we grow.

Crucially, not all connection is equal. Yale neuroscientists have found that in-person interaction produces significantly greater neural activity than video-based communication — with online exchanges suppressing the very brain signals that make us feel truly seen and understood.(5) Being in the room with someone, it turns out, is not just a preference. It is a neurological necessity.

If you think the key to being happy is a bigger bank account, there is a much easier solution. A Canadian study of 5,000 people found that *"doubling the number of friends in real life has an equivalent effect on wellbeing as a 50% increase in income"*. By contrast, the size of your online network did not matter for better wellbeing.(6) What does this mean for us in the modern world where social media is inevitable? The answer isn't necessarily social media abstinence, and whilst there is evidence that social media can increase stress, sadness and isolation, it can also be used to enhance connection, self-esteem and social connection.(7) The question isn't as simple as whether social media is good or bad, but how do we use this tool to enable real human connection?

## Using technology to build real connection

Technology is not the opposite of connection. Used well, it can be one of the ways we protect it. A message can remind someone they matter. A voice note can carry warmth across distance. A video call can help families stay close when geography gets in the way.

At its heart, connection is the experience of feeling seen, heard, understood, and valued by another. The most nourishing use of technology is active: checking in, arranging time together, joining communities, offering support, and turning online contact into real-world connection where possible. In this way, technology becomes a bridge rather than a barrier — not a substitute for human presence, but a tool that helps us create more of it.

My strongest message to you is **"digital connection should lead to deeper connection."** It should help us feel seen, supported and included — moving from passive scrolling to active connection. Here are a few ways to achieve it:

- Turn "we should catch up" into a date in the diary
- Use WhatsApp groups to organise walks, dinners or exercise classes
- Create "connection rituals" like a monthly dinner with friends

## The surprising link between connection and longevity

In one of the most fascinating studies in health research, researchers from Harvard followed hundreds of participants for more than 75 years tracking various aspects of their health and wellbeing.

After following people across decades of life, the study's central lesson is strikingly simple: our relationships are not separate from our health — they are part of it. As Robert Waldinger, director of the Harvard Study of Adult Development, puts it in his TED talk: *"Good relationships keep us happier and healthier. Period."*(8) The point is not simply that relationships make life more enjoyable. The study suggests that warm, dependable relationships are deeply connected with long-term health, resilience and ageing well. Those who were most satisfied in their relationship at age 50 were the healthiest at age 80.(9)

## The power of small moments

The good news is that meaningful connection does not always require deep conversations or lifelong friendships. Even brief interactions can make a difference.

Researchers describe something called *positivity resonance*, which occurs when people share positive emotions, mutual care, and genuine concern for one another. These interactions can strengthen relationships and contribute to better health.(10) Similarly, *micro-moments of connection* are short, authentic social exchanges that happen throughout daily life. A warm greeting from a neighbour, a friendly chat with a cashier, or a genuine smile from a colleague can create these moments that can have a measurable physiological effect and may contribute to better health and longevity over time.(11)

So perhaps the question is not only, "Are you connected?" but, "Who can you help feel more connected today?" A message, a phone call, a shared walk, or a few minutes of genuine attention may seem small — but these micro-moments are often the roots from which healthier, happier lives grow.
