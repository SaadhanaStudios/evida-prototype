## Daily stress

We live in a world where stress is often normalised — even expected — which makes it easy to overlook the toll it takes on our health. That's why understanding stress matters. Learning how to recognise it, understand its effects on the body, and develop strategies to manage it is essential. Particularly when stress becomes a daily companion. This kind of ongoing, or *chronic*, stress can quietly build in the body and mind over time and increases the risk of long-term health problems(1) if left unaddressed such as inflammation, poor sleep and fatigue.(2) The good news is that these can be addressed.

## Not all stress is harmful

Imagine your dog suddenly runs into a busy road. Your heart races, your breathing quickens, and your body floods with adrenaline. Then, moments later, your dog returns safely — and your system settles again. That surge is stress in action: a built-in survival response designed to protect you. Not all stress is harmful.

Stress can be broken down into three clear categories — only one of which is harmful. By breaking stress down into clear categories, we can better understand what our bodies are responding to and why.

1. **Eustress (Good Stress)** — This is positive, healthy stress. It helps motivate us; boosts focus and can improve performance — such as the stress felt before a big presentation or an important life event.

2. **Neustress (Neutral Stress)** — This is stress that we notice but that doesn't affect us directly, such as a volcano erupting in a faraway country. The information is registered by the brain, but without real significance, so the body doesn't react.

3. **Distress (Harmful Stress)** — This is the type of stress most of us associate with feeling overwhelmed. The situation, or "stressor", is perceived as a threat, triggering the body's stress response. When distress is frequent or ongoing, it can have a significantly negative impact on both physical and mental health.

## Stress: when physiology takes things personally

Distress is the type of stress we need to pay close attention to. Short-term, or *acute*, distress often comes on suddenly but settles just as quickly, allowing the body and mind to rebalance and return to a state of "homeostasis" — the body's natural state of internal balance.

Problems arise when bad stress doesn't switch off. When distress becomes persistent or long-term, it is known as *chronic stress*. This often develops in the context of repeated, everyday pressures, such as a relentlessly demanding job or ongoing personal or family challenges. In this state, the body remains on constant high alert, repeatedly releasing stress hormones such as cortisol and adrenaline as it prepares for perceived danger. When this response is triggered day after day, the body is given little opportunity to switch off and recover.

## Calming the stress response

The good news is that there are many techniques for handling stress better. Our bodies are remarkably adaptable, and even small, consistent changes can help calm the stress response and restore balance. Simple techniques can help regulate your nervous system before stress escalates. Over time, incorporating these tools into your routine can improve resilience and recovery. Reframing and box breathing are great examples.

- **Reframe:** You're getting ready to go on stage for a big presentation, your palms are sweaty, heart beat racing. Are you nervous or excited? That's up to you. It turns out the physiological response is very similar for both, and so you can reframe how you're feeling to either nervousness or excitement.(3) This reframe is often used to great effect by sports psychologists with elite athletes — including Pippa Grange with the England football team in 2018.(4)

- **Box Breathing:** Next time you feel your stress response rising, try this simple exercise for a 60 second reset. In just a few minutes, this pattern signals your body to shift out of a stress response, slowing your heart rate and promoting a sense of calm.

![Box Breathing Exercise diagram](/images/blog/box-breathing.png)

1. Inhale for 4 seconds
2. Hold for 4 seconds
3. Exhale for 4 seconds
4. Hold for 4 seconds
5. Repeat for 2–3 cycles, 2–3 times

## Building long-term resilience

Box breathing can be highly effective in the moment — alleviating the symptom of stress — but the root cause of stress often has multiple interconnected factors. For long term stress management lifestyle medicine is an excellent approach to reducing stress. By improving sleep, nutrition, physical activity, social connection and coping skills, the body and mind become more resilient to everyday pressures.(5) This in turn protects our health, improves our wellbeing and allows us to thrive.(6)

Stress is part of being human — but living in a constant state of stress shouldn't be normalised. The goal isn't to eliminate stress entirely, but to build a body and mind that can recover from it effectively. With the right habits, support, and awareness, resilience is something we can actively strengthen over time.

The best way to build resilience is with clarity. When you understand what is happening inside your body, you can make more precise, effective changes.
